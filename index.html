2. Pronoun Clarity: Ensuring Every Pronoun Has a Clear Home 代名詞の明確性：代名詞の指す対象を明確に

A pronoun (like it, they, he, its, their) is a word that replaces a noun. The noun it replaces is called the antecedent. On the SAT, every pronoun must logically and grammatically agree with its antecedent in number (singular or plural). This is especially important for students accustomed to Japanese, where pronouns are often omitted if the context is clear. English, and especially the SAT, demands absolute clarity.

The most common error tested is ambiguity, where a pronoun could refer to more than one noun in the sentence, creating confusion. The correct answer will always be the one that is the most clear and unambiguous.

Incorrect Example: The researcher showed the lab assistant the new data, hoping that he would find it insightful.

Corrected Version

The researcher showed the new data to the lab assistant, hoping that the assistant would find it insightful.

Explanation: In the incorrect version, the pronoun "he" is ambiguous. Who is "he"? The researcher or the assistant? The SAT considers this a critical error. The corrected version removes the ambiguity by replacing the pronoun with the specific noun it was intended to represent.

Pro Tip: The "Point and Name" Test. When you see a pronoun in an SAT question, physically point to the single word in the sentence that it refers to (the antecedent). Say that noun out loud in place of the pronoun. If you can't point to one specific word, or if the sentence sounds illogical when you substitute the noun, you've found an error. The SAT demands absolute clarity.

Practice Problems

Practice Problem 2.1

Easy

Although the new city regulations are strict, it has led to a significant decrease in traffic congestion.

Correct Answer: C

Explanation: The pronoun in the sentence refers to its antecedent, "regulations." Since "regulations" is a plural noun, the pronoun replacing it must also be plural ("they"). The verb must also agree with the plural subject, so "have" is correct. "They have" is the only choice that is both grammatically and logically correct.

  • (A) it has: "It" is singular and cannot refer to the plural "regulations."
  • (B) it have: "It" is singular.
  • (D) they has: "They" is plural, but "has" is a singular verb.
Difficulty Justification

This question is rated **Easy** because it tests the most fundamental aspect of pronoun-antecedent agreement: number. The antecedent ("regulations") is clearly plural and located close to the pronoun, making the singular/plural mismatch straightforward to identify.

Practice Problem 2.2

Hard

The committee argued with the council members about the new zoning laws, but they were ultimately unwilling to compromise.

Correct Answer: D

Explanation: The pronoun "they" in the original sentence is ambiguous. It is unclear whether "they" refers to "the committee" or "the council members." The SAT requires absolute clarity. To fix this error, the ambiguous pronoun must be replaced with the specific noun it is intended to represent. While the context doesn't make it 100% certain who was unwilling to compromise, only one answer choice provides the necessary specificity to resolve the grammatical ambiguity. Option (D) clarifies the sentence's meaning by naming one of the groups.

  • (A) NO CHANGE: Leaves the ambiguity unresolved.
  • (B) it was: "It" is singular and cannot refer to either plural group.
  • (C) the laws: It is illogical for the laws themselves to be "unwilling to compromise."
Difficulty Justification

This question is rated **Hard** because the error is not a simple grammatical mistake like number agreement, but a logical flaw of ambiguity. The original sentence sounds correct to the casual reader. Identifying that the lack of clarity is itself the error, and then choosing the option that provides specificity, is a higher-order skill tested in more difficult questions.

7. Punctuation Mastery: Colons (:) & Semicolons (;) 句読点のマスター：コロンとセミコロン

On the Digital SAT, colons and semicolons are not used for style; they follow strict grammatical rules. Both have one Golden Rule: the part of the sentence that comes before them must be a complete sentence (an independent clause). If you cannot place a period where the colon or semicolon is, the punctuation is incorrect.

  • Semicolon (;) = Period. A semicolon is used to connect two closely related complete sentences. For the SAT, you can treat it exactly like a period. If a period works, a semicolon works.
  • Colon (:) = "Here's what I mean." A colon is used after a complete sentence to introduce a list, an explanation, or a quotation that elaborates on the first sentence. It signals to the reader: "And here is the specific information I was just talking about."

Incorrect Example: The scientist needed equipment, such as: beakers, a microscope, and slides.

Corrected Version

The scientist needed several pieces of equipment: beakers, a microscope, and slides.

Explanation: In the incorrect version, the phrase "The scientist needed equipment, such as" is not a complete sentence, so it cannot be followed by a colon. The corrected version creates a complete sentence before the colon, satisfying the Golden Rule.

Pro Tip: The Period Test. When you see a colon or semicolon in the answer choices, check the clause before it. Can you replace the punctuation with a period and have a grammatically complete sentence? If YES, the colon/semicolon might be correct. If NO, it is 100% wrong. This single test will eliminate multiple answer choices instantly.

Practice Problems

Practice Problem 7.1

Easy

The ancient library contained texts on a wide range of subjects, including; history, philosophy, and astronomy.

Correct Answer: D

Explanation: The clause "The ancient library contained texts on a wide range of subjects" is a complete sentence (an independent clause). A colon is the correct punctuation to use after a complete sentence to introduce a list of examples. The other options introduce punctuation errors.

  • (A) and (B): It is always incorrect to place a semicolon or colon after an introductory phrase like "including" or "such as." The phrase before the punctuation must be a complete sentence on its own.
  • (C): This option is grammatically incorrect and does not properly set up the list.
Difficulty Justification

This question is rated **Easy** because it tests a classic SAT rule: using a colon to introduce a list. The independent clause is simple to identify, and the incorrect use of punctuation after "including" is a common trap that well-prepared students can easily spot.

Practice Problem 7.2

Hard

The results of the experiment were inconclusive, the data did not support the initial hypothesis, nor did it suggest an alternative.

Correct Answer: B

Explanation: The original sentence contains a "comma splice," which is a common SAT error. A comma splice occurs when two complete sentences (independent clauses) are joined together with only a comma.
• Clause 1: "The results of the experiment were inconclusive"
• Clause 2: "the data did not support the initial hypothesis, nor did it suggest an alternative"
Since both are complete sentences, they must be separated by a period, a comma + FANBOYS conjunction, or a semicolon. A semicolon is the perfect tool here to connect two closely related complete ideas.

  • (A) NO CHANGE: This is a comma splice.
  • (C) and (D): These options misuse semicolons and colons, creating new grammatical errors.
Difficulty Justification

This question is rated **Hard** because it requires the test-taker to first identify the subtle comma splice error in a complex sentence. Recognizing that "the data did not support..." begins a new independent clause is the key step. Many students will read past this error. The correct answer requires a precise application of the rule that a semicolon can function like a period to separate two complete thoughts.

14. Crafting Logical Transitions: Building a Coherent Argument 論理的な移行表現：一貫性のある議論の構築

Transition words and phrases (like However, Therefore, For example) are the logical glue that connects ideas. They act as signposts, telling the reader how one sentence relates to the next. On the SAT, choosing the correct transition is a test of pure logic, not vocabulary.

Your task is to read the sentences before and after the transition. Then, determine the relationship between them. Is the second sentence providing an example? A contrasting point? A result? Once you know the relationship, you can find the word that matches.

Common SAT Transition Functions:

  • Addition / Continuation: The second sentence adds a similar idea. (Furthermore, Moreover, In addition)
  • Contrast / Contradiction: The second sentence presents an opposing idea. (However, Nevertheless, In contrast)
  • Cause and Effect: The second sentence is a result of the first. (Therefore, Consequently, As a result)
  • Example / Emphasis: The second sentence gives a specific case or emphasizes a point. (For example, In fact, Indeed)

Pro Tip: Define the Relationship First. Cover the answer choices and read the sentences surrounding the blank. In your own words, state the connection. For example, say "This is a cause, and that is the effect." Then, uncover the choices and find the word that means 'cause and effect' (like Therefore). Don't let the answer choices confuse you before you've analyzed the logic.

Practice Problems

Practice Problem 14.1

Easy

The company invested heavily in new marketing campaigns. ____, its quarterly sales increased by over 50 percent.

Correct Answer: C

Explanation: To determine the correct transition, we must analyze the logical relationship between the two sentences. The first sentence states a cause (heavy investment in marketing). The second sentence states the effect of that action (a significant increase in sales). The word "Consequently" correctly signals this cause-and-effect relationship.

  • (A) Nevertheless: Signals a contrast, which is not present here.
  • (B) For example: Signals an example, but increased sales are a result, not an example, of the investment.
  • (D) Similarly: Signals a comparison, but the sentences describe a cause and its effect.
Difficulty Justification

This question is rated **Easy** because the cause-and-effect relationship between the two sentences is direct and unambiguous. This makes it a straightforward application of the core strategy for transition questions.

Practice Problem 14.2

Hard

Many critics argue that the novel's plot is derivative and uninspired. ____, the book has remained on the bestseller list for over a year, praised by readers for its emotional depth.

Correct Answer: D

Explanation: The logical relationship here is one of contrast or concession. The first sentence presents a negative viewpoint (critics' disapproval). The second sentence presents a contrasting positive outcome (commercial and popular success). "Nevertheless" is the ideal transition to signal this kind of unexpected result that occurs in spite of the preceding point. It essentially means "despite what was just said."

  • (A) Indeed: Used for emphasis, which would illogically suggest the book's success supports the critics' negative view.
  • (B) Therefore: Signals a result, which is the opposite of the relationship here.
  • (C) Furthermore: Adds a similar point, which is also incorrect.
Difficulty Justification

This question is rated **Hard** because the relationship is a nuanced form of contrast. Test-takers must understand that "Nevertheless" is used when a second point is true *despite* the first point being true. The other distractors are tempting if one only focuses on the second sentence (the book's success) without seeing its direct contradiction with the first sentence (the critics' opinion).
